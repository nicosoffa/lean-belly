I Pretended Not to Notice When My Granddaughter
Stopped Asking Me to Come Play. Until I Couldn't Pretend Anymore.

Diane spent seven years following every rule and gaining weight anyway. At 61, she finally found the explanation her doctors never gave her — and the four months that changed everything.

As told to Catherine Rhodes, Health Daily Report
Published May 20, 2026  ·  5 min read

Diane says the moment she knew something had to change was the day she made an excuse not to go to the park.

It was a Tuesday afternoon in September. My granddaughter Emma, who was seven at the time, ran into the living room and asked me if I wanted to come to the park with her and her mom.

I said I was tired.

Emma looked at me for a second — just a beat longer than normal — and then turned around and left. She didn't argue. She didn't ask again.

That was the moment I knew I had a problem. Not the number on the scale. Not the clothes that didn't fit. That look. She had already learned not to ask too hard.

I'm Diane Holloway. I'm 61, a retired high school principal from Tucson, Arizona. I had gained about 41 pounds over the previous seven years, despite doing everything I was told to do. I ate clean. I walked every day. I joined a gym when the walking stopped working. I tried intermittent fasting for four months. I gave up bread for a year.

Nothing worked. Or nothing kept working. I'd lose six pounds, gain eight. Lose four, gain five. The scale always drifted back up, and then a little further up.

"My doctor kept telling me my labs were 'borderline but fine.' I didn't feel fine. I felt like a stranger in my own body."

After that September afternoon, I started researching differently. Not looking for another diet. Looking for an explanation. Why was my body behaving as though every calorie I ate was a threat to be neutralized?

The Explanation My Doctors Never Gave Me

Six weeks later, a friend in my walking group shared an article she'd found. It was about mitochondrial efficiency — specifically how it declines in women after menopause at a rate that no caloric restriction can compensate for.

I'm not a scientist. But I've spent thirty years in education, so I know how to read research. I spent two evenings going through the studies. What I found was not a miracle cure. It was a mechanism — and it made complete sense of everything that had happened to my body over the previous seven years.

The short version: your mitochondria are the parts of your cells that decide what to do with the food you eat. When they're running efficiently, most of what you consume becomes energy. When their efficiency drops — which happens to women at an accelerated rate after 45 — more and more of what you eat gets converted into stored fat instead.

The math is brutal. A woman running at 65% mitochondrial efficiency can eat exactly the same as a woman running at 88% efficiency and store significantly more fat per day — without overeating a single calorie.

The critical insight: No diet addresses this. Cutting calories works on a 100%-efficiency body. On a 65%-efficiency body, you're still storing more than you're burning — just from a smaller intake. The mechanism, not the calories, is the variable that matters.

The research also identified something actionable: specific botanical compounds — resveratrol, EGCG, berberine, and three others — that have documented effects on restoring mitochondrial efficiency, particularly in postmenopausal women.

I wasn't looking for a supplement. But I was looking for those compounds. And I found a product that contained all six of them, in what appeared to be the right ratios.

What Happened Next

I ordered a three-month supply. I told myself I would be objective about it. I didn't change my diet. I didn't start a new exercise program. I wanted to isolate the variable — if this worked, I wanted to know it was the compound, not something else.

Week three: I woke up one morning and noticed I wasn't tired. I know how that sounds. But I had been tired every morning for years. I just assumed it was age. That morning, I wasn't.

Week six: I stepped on the scale for the first time since starting. I'd lost nine pounds. Without doing anything differently.

Month four: I was down 32 pounds.

My next physical showed metabolic markers that my doctor described as "surprisingly improved." She asked if I'd changed my diet significantly. I hadn't. She asked if I'd started a new exercise program. I hadn't. She was quiet for a moment and then said, "Well, keep doing whatever you're doing."

"I went back to the park with Emma that spring. We were there for two hours. I pushed her on the swings, chased her through the climbing structure, carried her on my shoulders when she got tired. She hasn't stopped asking me to come. That's the result that matters to me."
